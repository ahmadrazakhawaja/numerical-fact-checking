\begin{abstract}
Numerical claims are common in political discourse, news, and social media, and verifying them is
difficult even in one language. Multilingual verification is harder still. Large language models do
not always treat translated versions of the same claim consistently. As a result, a claim and its
translation may receive different reasoning-trace rankings and possibly different verdicts. This
thesis asks whether language invariance is useful as a \emph{training objective} for multilingual
reasoning, rather than only as a property to measure.

We study the question on CLEF CheckThat! 2026 Task~2, which requires ranking candidate reasoning
traces for numerical claims in English, Spanish, and Arabic. A verdict is then derived from the
top-ranked traces. We build on our shared-task submission: a Llama-3.1-8B trace scorer fine-tuned with
Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA), which ranked first on English and third on Spanish. We add a
score-level language-invariance objective. It penalises the model when it gives different normalised
relevance scores to corresponding reasoning traces across languages. We also introduce an evaluation
protocol that separates task accuracy from cross-lingual agreement and measures agreement at two
levels: the final verdict and the underlying trace ranking. We evaluate $28$ configurations across
three open-weight backbones on a held-out trilingual test set of $4{,}233$ parallel claims.

The results show a clear separation between ranking quality and ranking stability. Invariance training
does not change the official ranking metric: Recall@5 is statistically indistinguishable from standard
multilingual fine-tuning, and both approaches are close to the metric's attainable ceiling. However,
invariance training makes the rankings roughly four times more stable across languages. The stability
ranges of the invariance-trained and non-invariance systems do not overlap. Verdict quality also
improves modestly, mainly in Arabic and especially for the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class.
Verdict-level agreement is already high for every system and therefore does not distinguish between
them. Measuring consistency only from final answers would have missed the main effect. The objective
does not mainly change the label the model reaches; it changes whether the model relies on the same
evidence across languages.
\end{abstract}
