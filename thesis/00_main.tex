\begin{abstract}
Numerical claims are common in political discourse, news, and social media, and verifying them is
difficult even within a single language. In multilingual settings the difficulty compounds: large
language models frequently fail to treat semantically equivalent claims consistently, so a claim and
its translation may receive different reasoning-trace rankings and, in turn, different verdicts. This
thesis asks whether language invariance is a useful \emph{training objective} for multilingual
reasoning, or only a property worth measuring.

We study the question on CLEF CheckThat! 2026 Task~2, which requires ranking candidate reasoning
traces for numerical claims in English, Spanish, and Arabic and deriving a verdict from the top-ranked
traces. Building on our shared-task submission---a Llama-3.1-8B trace scorer fine-tuned with Quantized
Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) that ranked first on English and third on Spanish---we introduce a
score-level language-invariance objective that penalises a model when the normalised relevance scores
it assigns to the same reasoning traces diverge across languages, together with an evaluation protocol
that measures cross-lingual agreement independently of task accuracy and at two levels: the final
verdict and the underlying trace ranking. We evaluate $28$ configurations across three open-weight
backbones on a held-out trilingual test set of $4{,}233$ parallel claims.

The outcome is a separation rather than a uniform improvement. Invariance training leaves the official
ranking metric unchanged---Recall@5 is indistinguishable from standard multilingual fine-tuning, and
both approaches sit near the metric's attainable ceiling---while improving the cross-lingual stability
of the ranking roughly fourfold, with the invariance-trained and non-invariance populations not
overlapping at all. A modest gain in verdict quality accompanies it, concentrated in Arabic and within
Arabic in the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class. Verdict-level agreement, by contrast, is uniformly
high across every system we evaluate and fails to discriminate between them, so consistency measured
only over final answers would have missed the effect entirely. What the objective changes is not the
label a model arrives at, but whether it arrives there from the same evidence.
\end{abstract}
