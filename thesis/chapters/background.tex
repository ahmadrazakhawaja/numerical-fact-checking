\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section introduces the concepts and terminology that the rest of the thesis assumes. It is
written for a reader who is comfortable with general machine learning but not necessarily with large
language models, fact-checking, ranking, multilingual evaluation, or the numerical and statistical
tools we use. We begin with large language models and how they are adapted to downstream tasks, then
describe text ranking and the basic classification machinery on which our system is built. We then
introduce automated fact-checking and the specific numerical-claim-verification task studied here,
followed by the multilingual notions of cross-lingual transfer and language invariance that motivate
the thesis, a short treatment of numerical reasoning in language models, and finally the evaluation
metrics and agreement statistics used throughout. Readers already familiar with a given topic can
safely skip the corresponding subsection; the later sections cross-reference the definitions
established here.

\subsection{Large Language Models}
\label{subsec:bg-llm}

A \emph{large language model} (LLM) is a neural network trained to model the probability of natural
language text. Modern LLMs are built on the \emph{Transformer} architecture~\cite{vaswani2017attention} and are
typically \emph{decoder-only}: they are trained by \emph{next-token prediction}, that is, to predict
the next unit of text given all the preceding units. Formally, for a sequence of tokens $x_1,\dots,x_T$ the
model factorizes the joint probability as $\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ and is optimized
to maximize this likelihood over a very large corpus. After this \emph{pre-training} stage the model
has absorbed broad linguistic and world knowledge, which can then be steered toward specific tasks.

\paragraph{Tokens and tokenization.}
LLMs do not operate on characters or whole words but on \emph{tokens}: sub-word units produced by a
\emph{tokenizer}. A tokenizer is learned on the pre-training corpus and splits text into frequent
fragments, so that common words become single tokens while rare words are broken into several pieces.
This \emph{subword} scheme---most commonly byte-pair encoding~\cite{sennrich2016bpe}---keeps the
vocabulary finite while still being able to represent any string.
Tokenization is not a neutral detail for this thesis: the way a tokenizer splits numbers and
non-Latin scripts directly affects how well a model can reason about quantities across languages, as
discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:bg-numeracy}.

\paragraph{Instruction tuning.}
A raw pre-trained model predicts plausible continuations but does not reliably follow instructions.
\emph{Instruction tuning} is a further training stage in which the model is fine-tuned on
(instruction, response) examples so that it learns to carry out tasks phrased as natural-language
requests. The resulting \emph{instruction-tuned} models---for example
Mistral-7B-Instruct~\cite{jiang2023mistral7b}, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct~\cite{llama31instruct}, and
Qwen3-8B~\cite{qwen3technicalreport}, the three \emph{backbones} used in this thesis---can be prompted
to rank items, classify text, or answer questions without any task-specific training. The number in
each name (7B, 8B) refers to the count of trainable parameters in billions, which is one rough proxy
for model capacity.

\subsection{Adapting Language Models to a Task}
\label{subsec:bg-adapt}

There are two broad ways to make an LLM perform a particular task: \emph{prompt} it without changing
its weights, or \emph{fine-tune} some or all of its weights on task data. The thesis uses both, so we
introduce each.

\paragraph{Prompting and in-context learning.}
In \emph{zero-shot} prompting the model is simply given an instruction and the input, and asked to
produce the output; in \emph{few-shot} prompting the prompt additionally contains a handful of solved
examples, from which the model infers the pattern---a behaviour known as \emph{in-context learning}.
Prompting requires no training and no gradient updates, which makes it a natural \emph{baseline}, but
its behaviour is sensitive to the exact wording and formatting of the prompt.

\paragraph{Chain-of-thought reasoning.}
A particularly important prompting technique for this thesis is \emph{chain-of-thought} (CoT)
prompting, in which the model is encouraged to produce a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps
before committing to its final answer, rather than emitting the answer directly~\cite{WeiWSB23}.
Making the reasoning explicit in this way substantially improves performance on tasks that require
multiple steps, such as arithmetic and multi-hop questions, and it also makes the model's reasoning
inspectable. Chain-of-thought is directly relevant here because the ``reasoning traces'' that our task
asks a system to rank (Section~\ref{subsec:bg-factcheck}) are exactly such chains of thought: each
trace is a step-by-step argument, generated by a language model, that works through a claim and its
evidence and ends in a proposed verdict. The thesis does not generate these traces; it takes them as
given and studies how to \emph{select} and \emph{rank} the most reliable ones.

\paragraph{Full fine-tuning versus parameter-efficient fine-tuning.}
\emph{Fine-tuning} continues training the model on labelled task data. Updating all parameters of a
7--8B model (\emph{full fine-tuning}) is expensive in memory and compute. \emph{Parameter-efficient
fine-tuning} (PEFT) instead freezes the pre-trained weights and trains only a small number of new
parameters. The most common PEFT method is \emph{Low-Rank Adaptation}
(LoRA)~\cite{hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge}: for each adapted weight matrix, LoRA learns a pair of
small low-rank matrices whose product is added to the frozen weight, so that only these compact
\emph{adapters} are trained. The number of trainable parameters is controlled by the adapter
\emph{rank} $r$, and the update is scaled by a factor $\alpha$.

\paragraph{Quantization and QLoRA.}
\emph{Quantization} stores model weights at lower numerical precision (for example 4~bits instead of
16 or 32) to reduce memory. \emph{Quantized LoRA} (QLoRA)~\cite{dettmers2023qloraefficientfinetuningquantized}
combines the two ideas: the frozen base model is loaded in 4-bit precision while the LoRA adapters are
trained on top of it in higher precision. This makes it feasible to fine-tune models with billions of
parameters on a modest GPU budget, which is the regime in which our experiments operate.
Figure~\ref{fig:lora-qlora} illustrates how the low-rank adapters are added alongside the frozen
weights.

\paragraph{Preference optimization.}
Beyond supervised fine-tuning, models can be trained from \emph{preferences}: pairs of a preferred and
a rejected output for the same input. \emph{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback} (RLHF) was the
original approach, but it is complex because it requires training a separate reward model and running
reinforcement learning. \emph{Direct Preference Optimization}
(DPO)~\cite{rafailov2024directpreferenceoptimizationlanguage} achieves a similar effect more simply: it
directly adjusts the model to raise the likelihood of preferred outputs relative to rejected ones,
while staying close to a reference model, without an explicit reward model or reinforcement learning.
The thesis uses DPO as one of its ranking baselines.

\subsection{Text Ranking with Language Models}
\label{subsec:bg-ranking}

\emph{Ranking} is the task of ordering a set of candidate items by how well they satisfy some
criterion; the closely related task of \emph{re-ranking} reorders an already-retrieved candidate set.
LLMs can be used as rankers in three standard formulations, which differ in how many candidates the
model sees at once; Figure~\ref{fig:ranking-formulations} contrasts them.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pointwise.} Each candidate is scored independently, and the ranking is obtained by
sorting the scores. This is the formulation our trace scorer uses.
\item \textbf{Pairwise.} The model compares two candidates at a time and states which is better; a
global ordering is assembled from the accumulated pairwise
preferences~\cite{qin2024largelanguagemodelseffective}. This keeps each decision simple but requires a
number of comparisons that grows quadratically with the number of candidates.
\item \textbf{Listwise.} All candidates are presented in a single prompt and the model emits a complete
ordering. This is efficient at inference but couples the decision to the whole input, including the
order in which candidates are listed~\cite{liu-etal-2025-lipo}.
\end{itemize}

  \caption{The three standard formulations for ranking candidates with an LLM. \emph{Pointwise}
  scoring evaluates each candidate independently and sorts by score (the formulation used by our
  trace scorer); \emph{pairwise} ranking compares two candidates at a time and orders them by
  accumulated wins; \emph{listwise} ranking presents all candidates in one prompt and reads off the

\paragraph{LLM-as-a-judge and presentation bias.}
When an LLM is used to \emph{evaluate} or compare candidates, the setting is often called
\emph{LLM-as-a-judge}. A recurring problem is that such judgments are influenced by factors unrelated
to content quality. \emph{Positional} (or \emph{position}) bias is the tendency to prefer a candidate
because of \emph{where} it appears in the prompt rather than \emph{what} it says; related effects
include verbosity bias and sensitivity to prompt
format~\cite{shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy,qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect}. These
biases matter for ranking because they can determine which candidate is chosen independently of its
actual reliability, and they are one reason the thesis moves from generative ranking toward
independently scored candidates.

\subsection{Classification and Training Objectives}
\label{subsec:bg-classification}

Our system reformulates ranking as \emph{binary classification}, so we recall the relevant basics. A
classifier maps an input to one or more real-valued \emph{logits}. For \emph{binary} classification
one can use a single logit $\ell$ passed through the \emph{sigmoid} function
$\sigma(\ell) = 1/(1+e^{-\ell})$ to produce a probability, or two logits
$(\ell^{\text{neg}}, \ell^{\text{pos}})$ passed through the \emph{softmax} function, which normalizes a
vector of logits into a probability distribution. The model is trained by minimizing a \emph{loss}
that penalizes disagreement between the predicted probability and the true label; for classification
this is the \emph{cross-entropy} loss (its two-class special case is \emph{binary cross-entropy}). The
higher the probability the model assigns to the positive class, the higher we can consider its
\emph{score} for that input, and this score is exactly what we later sort on to obtain a ranking.
A \emph{classification head} is the small final layer that turns the model's internal representation
of an input into these logits.

\subsection{Automated Fact-Checking and Numerical Claim Verification}
\label{subsec:bg-factcheck}

\emph{Automated fact-checking} is the task of assessing whether a \emph{claim}---a statement that can
be true or false---is supported by evidence. The step that assigns a truth value to a claim given
evidence is called \emph{claim verification} (or veracity prediction), and it is the part of the
fact-checking pipeline this thesis is concerned with. It is worth distinguishing it from the earlier
stages of a full fact-checking system, which typically also include \emph{claim detection} (deciding
which sentences are checkworthy claims) and \emph{evidence retrieval} (finding documents relevant to a
claim); we take claim detection as already done and focus on verification given retrieved
evidence~\cite{sahitaj2025automatedfactcheckingrealworldclaims}.

\paragraph{Core components: claims, evidence, and verdicts.}
A verification example has three core components. The \emph{claim} is the statement to be checked. The
\emph{evidence} is a set of retrieved text snippets---the output of a \emph{retrieval} step that
searches a document collection for material relevant to the claim---against which the claim is
assessed; in this thesis the evidence is provided with each example, so we do not perform retrieval
ourselves. The \emph{verdict} (also called a veracity label) is the categorical outcome describing the
claim's relationship to the evidence, drawn here from the label space \textsf{True}, \textsf{False},
and \textsf{Conflicting}. In our specific task a fourth component is added on top of these: a set of
candidate \emph{reasoning traces} (chains of thought, in the sense of
Section~\ref{subsec:bg-adapt}), each carrying its own proposed verdict, which the system must rank.
\emph{Numerical claims} are claims whose truth hinges on quantities---counts, dates, percentages,
rankings, or comparisons---and they are especially demanding, because a small change in a number, a
unit, or the scope of a comparison can flip the verdict~\cite{v2024quantemprealworldopendomainbenchmark}.

\paragraph{Reasoning traces and verifier-guided verification.}
A recent approach to hard verification problems is \emph{test-time scaling}: instead of producing a
single answer, the model generates several independent \emph{reasoning traces}---step-by-step
chains of reasoning, each ending in a proposed verdict---and a separate component decides which traces
to trust~\cite{chungkham2025thinkrightmoretesttime}. The component that selects or ranks traces is a
\emph{verifier}, and the overall setup is \emph{verifier-guided}. The task studied in this thesis
follows exactly this shape: for each claim it provides evidence and a set of candidate reasoning
traces with their per-trace verdicts, and a system must (i) \emph{rank} the traces by how useful they
are for reaching the correct answer and (ii) derive a final \emph{claim-level verdict}, typically from
the highest-ranked traces. Because the traces are given and only their ranking and aggregation are
learned, the task is best seen as a ranking-and-aggregation problem rather than free-form reasoning.
Figure~\ref{fig:factcheck-pipeline} shows the full pipeline and marks which parts are provided by the
task and which are learned in this thesis.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and the shared task.}
QuanTemp is a benchmark of real-world numerical claims with associated evidence, introduced to study
this problem in an open-domain setting~\cite{v2024quantemprealworldopendomainbenchmark}. The
CLEF~CheckThat!~2026 Task~2 shared task~\cite{clef-checkthat:2026:task2} adopts the verifier-guided
formulation for \emph{multilingual} numerical claim verification across English, Spanish, and Arabic,
and is the setting in which our system~\cite{khawaja2026gipplab} was developed. More broadly,
multilingual fact-checking has its own line of benchmarks---for example
X-Fact~\cite{gupta2021xfactnewbenchmarkdataset} and XFEVER~\cite{chang2023xfeverexploringfactverification}---which
consistently report that transferring verification across languages is difficult and that performance
varies widely by language.

\subsection{Multilinguality and Cross-Lingual Consistency}
\label{subsec:bg-multilingual}

A \emph{multilingual} model is one trained on text in many languages; the practical hope is
\emph{cross-lingual transfer}, where ability learned in one language carries over to others. A large
body of work shows that multilingual models learn substantially \emph{shared} internal
representations, so that semantically equivalent inputs in different languages are encoded similarly.
For decoder-only LLMs, interpretability studies suggest that models process non-English input through
intermediate representations that are close to English before mapping back to the input language, an
effect described as operating in a \emph{latent language}~\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent,schut2025multilingualllmsthinkenglish}.
This shared space is what makes it plausible, in principle, for a model to behave the same way
regardless of the language of the input.

\paragraph{Consistency and language invariance.}
In practice, however, models are frequently \emph{not} consistent. \emph{Consistency} is defined as
invariance of a model's predictions under meaning-preserving changes to the input: if two inputs mean
the same thing, a consistent model produces non-contradictory
outputs~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained}. When the meaning-preserving change is
a \emph{translation}, consistency becomes \emph{cross-lingual consistency}, or \emph{language
invariance}: agreement of a model's outputs across translations of the same
input~\cite{Fierro_2022,Qi_2023}. This is the central property the thesis studies. A crucial subtlety,
which the thesis takes care to respect, is that \textbf{consistency is decoupled from accuracy}: a
model can be consistently wrong (agreeing across languages on an incorrect answer) or accurate but
inconsistent (right on average yet disagreeing with itself across
languages)~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023}. A further caveat is that
answering the same way across languages does not by itself prove the knowledge is stored in a shared
representation~\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}. Because of this decoupling,
invariance and accuracy must be \emph{measured separately} before their relationship can be studied,
which directly shapes the evaluation protocol of this thesis.

\subsection{Numerical Reasoning in Language Models}
\label{subsec:bg-numeracy}

\emph{Numeracy}---the ability to reason about numbers---is a known weak point of language models.
Probing studies report that models have a fragile ``number sense'' and fail on elementary numerical
operations~\cite{rahman2025fragilenumbersenseprobing,wallace2019nlpmodelsknownumbers}. Part of the
difficulty is representational: the \emph{magnitude} of a number (how big it is) is not explicitly
encoded by standard token embeddings, and subword tokenization can split a numeral into pieces that
obscure its value~\cite{wallace2019nlpmodelsknownumbers}. Two lines of remedy are relevant here.
\emph{Value-aware} representations encode the numeric \emph{value} of a span rather than relying only
on its surface tokens~\cite{dutulescu2026valueawarenumericalrepresentationstransformer,schwartz2024numerologicnumberencodingenhanced}.
Separately, the numeral \emph{script} and \emph{format} themselves matter across languages: the same
quantity written in Arabic-Indic digits or with different decimal and thousands separators tokenizes
differently, and accuracy can drop for under-represented scripts even when the underlying arithmetic
is unchanged~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm,bhattacharya2025investigatinginteractionlinguisticmathematical}.
This is why numeral script is an independent axis of cross-lingual variation, and why the thesis pays
particular attention to it for Arabic.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Agreement Statistics}
\label{subsec:bg-metrics}

Finally, we define the metrics used to report results. They fall into three groups: classification
quality, ranking quality, and cross-lingual agreement.

\paragraph{Classification metrics.}
For a class of interest, \emph{precision} is the fraction of items predicted to be in the class that
truly are, and \emph{recall} is the fraction of true class members that were predicted. The
\emph{F1 score} is their harmonic mean, $\mathrm{F1} = 2\,PR/(P+R)$, which balances the two.
\emph{Macro-F1} averages the F1 score computed separately for each class, giving every class equal
weight regardless of how frequent it is; we use it for the claim-level verdict, which has three
classes.

\paragraph{Ranking metrics.}
For a ranked list and a set of \emph{relevant} items, \emph{Recall@$k$} is the fraction of relevant
items that appear in the top $k$ positions, and \emph{Precision@$k$} is the fraction of the top $k$
positions occupied by relevant items. \emph{Mean Reciprocal Rank at $k$} (MRR@$k$) rewards placing a
relevant item early: it is the reciprocal of the rank of the first relevant item (or zero if none
appears in the top $k$), averaged over queries. In our task, a trace is ``relevant'' when its verdict
matches the gold claim label, and Recall@5 is the primary ranking metric.

\paragraph{Inter-rater agreement.}
To quantify how much two or more sets of predictions agree---for example, the verdicts a model
produces for the same claim in different languages---we borrow \emph{inter-rater agreement} statistics
from the study of human annotators, treating each language as a ``rater.'' The simplest measure is
\emph{raw agreement}, the fraction of items on which the raters give the same label, but this rewards
agreement that would occur by chance. Chance-corrected coefficients address this: \emph{Cohen's
$\kappa$} measures agreement between exactly two raters beyond
chance~\cite{cohen1960coefficient}; \emph{Fleiss' $\kappa$} generalizes this to more than two
raters~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}; and \emph{Krippendorff's $\alpha$} is a reliability coefficient
that also tolerates missing data~\cite{krippendorff2004content}. All three equal $1$ for perfect
agreement and around $0$ for chance-level agreement.

\paragraph{Rank-agreement.}
When the outputs being compared are \emph{rankings} rather than labels, we use rank-correlation
statistics. \emph{Kendall's $\tau$} compares two orderings by the balance of \emph{concordant} and
\emph{discordant} pairs (pairs ordered the same versus oppositely)~\cite{kendall1938new}, and
\emph{Spearman's $\rho$} measures the correlation between the two sets of
ranks~\cite{spearman1904proof}. Both range from $-1$ (reversed) through $0$ (unrelated) to $+1$
(identical order). To focus on the top of the ranking, we also use the \emph{Jaccard} overlap of the
two top-$k$ sets, defined as the size of their intersection divided by the size of their union. These
rank-agreement measures let us ask not only whether a model reaches the same verdict across languages,
but whether it ranks the underlying reasoning traces in the same way.
