\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Numerical claims are pervasive in political discourse, news reporting, and social
media. They typically involve quantities, dates, percentages, rankings, or
comparisons, and small shifts in scope or numerical interpretation can change a
claim's veracity entirely. This makes numerical claim verification considerably
more demanding than standard textual entailment, because a system must reason
jointly over linguistic context and quantitative relationships rather than over
surface semantics alone~\cite{mishra2022numglue, rahman2025fragile}. The
challenge is amplified in multilingual settings, where the same quantitative
assertion may appear in several languages and where a reliable verifier must
preserve numerical meaning across them~\cite{huang2024multilingual}.

Large language models (LLMs) have recently emerged as strong tools for the
reasoning and ranking components of automated fact-checking, and have been used
both as rankers of candidate evidence and as judges of reasoning
quality~\cite{sahitaj2025automated, qin2024pairwise}. However, a growing body of
work shows that their judgments are not language-invariant: semantically
equivalent inputs may receive different rankings or verdicts depending solely on
the language in which they are expressed~\cite{fu2025reliable}. Prompting-based
rankers additionally suffer from systematic biases such as position and verbosity
bias, which further destabilise their outputs~\cite{shi2025judging}. These
observations sit in tension with evidence that LLMs encode partly
language-independent internal representations~\cite{stolle2026reasoning}, raising
the question of why such latent consistency does not translate into consistent
external behaviour.

This tension motivates the central concern of this thesis. If a model can be
encouraged to produce consistent judgments across languages, does that consistency
reflect genuinely more robust reasoning, or merely more stable surface behaviour
without any underlying gain in quality? In other words, is language invariance a
useful \emph{objective} to optimise for, or only a property worth measuring?

\subsection{Problem Statement and Research Gap}
\label{sec:problem}

Two strands of recent work bear directly on this question but have not been
studied together. First, preference optimisation methods such as Direct
Preference Optimization (DPO) have been shown to improve the quality of
LLM-based evaluation by aligning model outputs with preferred
judgments~\cite{rafailov2023dpo}. Yet their effect on the \emph{cross-lingual
consistency} of those judgments is not well understood: it remains unclear
whether optimising preferences---whether on English-only or on genuinely
multilingual data---makes a model more invariant across languages, or simply
better in the language it was tuned on. Second, because numerical magnitude is
not explicitly encoded by standard subword tokenisation, LLMs frequently
struggle to reason reliably about quantities~\cite{rahman2025numberland}.
Value-aware numerical representations have been proposed to address this
limitation~\cite{dutulescu2026value}, and numerical reasoning is itself an
attractive probe for genuine understanding because it grounds evaluation in
objective quantities that are invariant across language and
phrasing~\cite{lewkowycz2022solving}. However, whether such representations
improve cross-lingual robustness, rather than only monolingual numerical
accuracy, is an open question.

There is therefore a lack of systematic understanding of how preference
optimisation and improved numerical representations interact with language
invariance in multilingual numerical fact-checking. This thesis addresses that
gap directly, treating language invariance not only as a property to be measured
but as an explicit target that training objectives and input representations can
be designed to promote.

\subsection{Grounding: CLEF CheckThat! 2026 Task 2}
\label{sec:grounding}

We study these questions in the concrete setting of CLEF CheckThat! 2026
Task~2 on multilingual numerical claim verification~\cite{vlachos2026checkthat}.
The task adopts a test-time scaling formulation for fact
verification~\cite{chungkham2025think}: for each claim and its retrieved
evidence, multiple reasoning traces are generated by an LLM under varying
temperatures to induce diverse reasoning paths, redundant traces are removed,
and the participant must train a verifier that ranks the remaining traces by
their utility for reaching the correct verdict and then derives a final
claim-level verdict from the top-ranked traces. The claims are drawn from the
QuanTemp benchmark together with Spanish and Arabic claims from the previous
CLEF edition, and are provided in English, Spanish, and
Arabic~\cite{v2024quantemp}. Ranking quality is measured with Recall@5 and
MRR@5 over traces whose verdict matches the gold label, while claim-verdict
prediction is evaluated with macro-averaged F1; the final per-language score is
the average of Recall@5 and macro-F1.

The thesis builds on our own participation in this shared task. Our submitted
system fine-tunes \texttt{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} with Quantized Low-Rank
Adaptation (QLoRA)~\cite{dettmers2023qlora, meta2024llama, hu2021lora} as a
multilingual supervised trace scorer that scores each claim--trace pair
independently and ranks traces by their predicted relevance. This formulation
proved more controllable than generative ranking and ranked first on English and
third on Spanish on the official leaderboard, providing a competitive baseline
and an established experimental pipeline on which the present work extends.

Figure~\ref{fig:language-invariance} illustrates the core problem. The same
claim, together with its evidence and candidate reasoning traces, is presented to
the trace scorer in English, Spanish, and Arabic. A language-variant model can
return divergent rankings and verdicts for these semantically equivalent inputs,
whereas a language-invariant model should return consistent ones. Quantifying and
reducing this divergence---and determining whether reducing it also improves
ranking quality---is the empirical heart of this thesis.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

Guided by the gap described above, this thesis investigates the relationship
between language invariance and reasoning quality in multilingual numerical
fact-checking through three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does optimising for language-invariant reasoning improve
    ranking performance in multilingual numerical fact-checking?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] What is the relationship between language invariance and
    reasoning quality across different languages?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Can language-invariant training objectives lead to more
    robust multilingual reasoning models?
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Cutting across these questions is a methodological sub-thread on
representation: namely, to what extent value-aware numerical representations,
which expose quantitative magnitude more explicitly than ordinary subword
tokenisation, contribute to cross-lingual robustness in numerically grounded
reasoning. We treat this as a lens on RQ2 and RQ3 rather than as a separate
question, since numerical grounding is precisely where language invariance is
most testable.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

In addressing these questions, this thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item It frames language invariance as an explicit training objective---rather
    than only an evaluation criterion---for multilingual numerical claim
    verification, and operationalises it on a competitive trace-scoring pipeline
    derived from our CLEF CheckThat! 2026 Task~2 system.
    \item It provides a systematic comparison of ranking formulations through the
    lens of cross-lingual consistency, spanning prompting-based ranking, Direct
    Preference Optimization under English-only and multilingual preference data,
    and supervised trace scoring.
    \item It investigates whether value-aware numerical representations improve
    cross-lingual robustness, isolating the effect of explicit numerical encoding
    from that of multilingual training.
    \item It evaluates these settings on parallel English, Spanish, and Arabic
    data using both the standard shared-task metrics (Recall@5, MRR@5, and
    macro-F1) and direct measures of cross-lingual agreement between model
    judgments on semantically equivalent inputs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Thesis Outline}
\label{sec:outline}

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
\textbf{Chapter~2} reviews related work on LLM-based ranking and
fact-checking, language invariance and multilingual evaluation, preference
optimisation, and numerical reasoning, situating our contributions within each.
\textbf{Chapter~3} describes the CLEF CheckThat! 2026 Task~2 setting, the
multilingual datasets, and the evaluation metrics, including the construction of
the parallel English, Spanish, and Arabic data used for our invariance
experiments.
\textbf{Chapter~4} presents the methodology, covering the trace-scoring
baseline, the preference-optimisation and language-invariant training objectives,
and the value-aware numerical representations.
\textbf{Chapter~5} reports the experimental setup and results across the three
languages.
\textbf{Chapter~6} discusses the findings in relation to the research questions,
and \textbf{Chapter~7} concludes and outlines directions for future work.
