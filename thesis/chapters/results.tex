\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

This chapter reports and interprets the experimental results. We first describe the evaluation setup
and explain which runs are included in the analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:res-setup}). We then report
task performance (Section~\ref{subsec:res-task}) and cross-lingual consistency
(Section~\ref{subsec:res-consistency}) separately. Section~\ref{subsec:res-invariance} brings these
results together to answer RQ1. Section~\ref{subsec:res-language} identifies where the cross-lingual
differences occur, and Section~\ref{subsec:res-ablations} examines the invariance weight, numeric
normalisation, and backbone families. Section~\ref{subsec:res-relationship} analyses the relationship
between invariance and reasoning quality (RQ2). Finally, Section~\ref{subsec:res-threats} discusses
the threats to validity, including a context-budget confound, and Section~\ref{subsec:res-summary}
summarises the findings for all three research questions.

\subsection{Experimental Scope and Data Hygiene}
\label{subsec:res-setup}

All results use the held-out \emph{test} set in the aligned trilingual form described in
Section~\ref{subsec:method-data} and summarised in Table~\ref{tab:dataset}. It contains $4{,}233$
claims, each available in English, Spanish, and Arabic with parallel trace sets of $20$ candidate
traces per claim. Task metrics are computed separately for each language, consistency metrics compare
the three language-conditioned outputs for the same claim. The test set
is separate from the training and validation splits. No configuration was trained on it, and early
stopping and best-epoch selection used only the validation split. The results therefore measure
generalisation to unseen claims. Each configuration was trained and evaluated with three random
seeds, and we report the mean. In total, the study contains $32$ configurations, prompted baselines,
earlier trace scorers, standard multilingual fine-tunes, and the invariance-trained models introduced
in this thesis.

\paragraph{Excluded runs.}
Four of the $32$ configurations are excluded from the statistics below. Three rows \emph{Mistral,
English DPO}, \emph{Llama, inv.\ $\lambda^\star$=0.05}, and \emph{Qwen, prompted} report the same
scores to four decimal places, identical across all three languages, with every cross-lingual
agreement measure exactly $1.000$. Inspecting their outputs explains the pattern, these runs
degenerated to emitting the input order, returning the identity permutation $[0,1,2,\dots]$ for every
claim instead of a content-based ranking. A ranking that ignores the input is by definition the same
in every language, so on parallel data it yields identical per-language scores and perfect
cross-lingual agreement. The resulting numbers describe the failure mode, not the systems, and the
agreement of exactly $1.000$ measures nothing at all. We therefore exclude these rows from every
statistic and report them only for reference in Tables~\ref{tab:results-summary}
and~\ref{tab:results-main}. The remaining analysis contains $28$ configurations.

Direct Preference Optimization is a special case worth stating explicitly. It was attempted only in
the single English configuration reported here. That run degenerated in the way just described and
showed no advantage over the supervised trace scorer, so the approach was not developed further: no
multilingual DPO variants, no additional seeds, and no hyper-parameter search were run. It appears in
the tables as a record of the attempt rather than as a tuned baseline, and the comparison between
preference-based and score-based ranking in this thesis should be read accordingly.

The fourth exclusion has a different reason and illustrates the central measurement principle of this
thesis. The \emph{random ranking} baseline also achieves
perfect cross-lingual agreement Fleiss $\kappa = 1.000$ and Kendall $\tau = 1.000$ on every language
pair. This is not an artefact, the baseline ranks traces in a fixed seeded permutation of trace
indices that does not depend on the input at all, so it produces literally the same ranking in every
language. It is therefore \emph{invariant by construction}. However, it is also at the bottom of
every accuracy measure. This shows that a system can be perfectly invariant while carrying no useful
information. Invariance must therefore be reported alongside task accuracy, never instead of
it~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023}. We exclude the random baseline
from correlation statistics because its perfect agreement would dominate them without adding useful
information. We retain it in Table~\ref{tab:results-summary} as the accuracy floor.

\paragraph{Achievable ceiling.}
As established in Section~\ref{subsec:method-task}, Recall@5 has a structural upper bound. On this
test set the bound is $0.3005$ (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). In addition, $578$ of the $4{,}233$ claims ($13.7\%$) have no
candidate trace whose verdict matches the gold label. These claims receive zero Recall@5 regardless
of the ranking. The Recall@5 results below should therefore be compared with $0.3005$, not with $1.0$.

\paragraph{Terminology.}
The task uses two metrics that behave differently, so we keep them separate. \emph{Verdict quality}
means claim-level macro-F1, while \emph{ranking quality} means Recall@5. When one summary of task
performance is needed, we use \emph{mean macro-F1}, verdict macro-F1 averaged across English,
Spanish, and Arabic. This is also what \emph{accuracy} means below. It never refers to Recall@5 or the
official combined score. \emph{Consistency} refers only to cross-lingual agreement measures such as
Fleiss $\kappa$, Cohen's $\kappa$, and Kendall $\tau$. These are calculated independently of both task
metrics.

\subsection{Task Performance}
\label{subsec:res-task}

Table~\ref{tab:results-summary} condenses the results, for each training group it reports the two
best configurations by mean macro-F1, placing accuracy and consistency side by side. Group averages
over all configurations are quoted in the text where they support a statistical claim. Per-model results for all $28$ configurations are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:results-full} (Tables~\ref{tab:results-main} and~\ref{tab:results-consistency}).
Several observations follow.

\input{tables/table-results-summary}

\paragraph{Every trained system beats the baselines, but the margins are narrow.}
The random ranking baseline reaches $0.2181$ Recall@5 and $0.5820$ macro-F1 in English. The zero-shot
prompted baselines are barely different, the Llama prompted baseline scores $0.2234$ and $0.5751$.
This confirms, on a larger and fully parallel evaluation set, the shared-task finding that zero-shot
generative ranking adds little over chance in this setting. Fine-tuned trace scorers improve
substantially on macro-F1, reaching up to $0.6263$ in English. Their Recall@5 improvement is smaller,
reaching up to $0.2710$.

\paragraph{Ranking headroom is nearly exhausted, verdict quality is not.}
The best Recall@5, $0.2710$, is roughly $90\%$ of the achievable ceiling of $0.3005$. The ranking
component is therefore close to saturated for this candidate-trace set. Further gains will require
candidate sets with more gold-matching traces, not only better ordering of the current traces.
Macro-F1, by contrast, ranges from $0.5715$ to $0.6320$, so modelling choices still matter for
verdict quality. The official score averages these two metrics and is therefore increasingly driven
by the verdict term.

\paragraph{Metric redundancy.}
Across the $28$ configurations, Recall@5, Precision@5, MRR@5, macro-F1, and the per-class F1 scores
for \textsf{True} and \textsf{False} are almost collinear. Their Pearson correlations range from
$0.92$ to $0.99$. F1 for the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class is the exception, with correlations
of only $r=0.61$--$0.85$. Most headline metrics therefore produce nearly the same ordering of
systems. The \textsf{Conflicting} class is what separates them. Section~\ref{subsec:res-language}
shows that this class also carries the cross-lingual effect.

\subsection{Cross-Lingual Consistency}
\label{subsec:res-consistency}

The right-hand block of Table~\ref{tab:results-summary} reports consistency independently of accuracy,
Table~\ref{tab:results-consistency} gives the per-model values. The two types of consistency measure
behave very differently. Only ranking agreement clearly distinguishes between systems.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{results-agreement}
  \caption{Verdict agreement versus ranking agreement for the best configuration in each training
  group, selected by mean macro-F1. Fleiss $\kappa$ over the three language-conditioned verdicts is
  high for every group and separates them weakly. Mean pairwise Kendall $\tau$ between the
  language-conditioned trace rankings separates the same systems by almost an order of magnitude.}
  \label{fig:results-agreement}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Verdicts agree across languages, rankings do not.}
Fleiss $\kappa$ over the language-conditioned verdicts is high and falls within a narrow range, on
average $0.8596$ for the earlier trace scorers, $0.9028$ for standard fine-tuning, and $0.9443$ for
invariance-trained models. Figure~\ref{fig:results-agreement} shows the same pattern for the best
configuration in each group. Based only on verdict agreement, every system
appears strongly language-invariant. Kendall $\tau$ between the trace \emph{rankings} gives a very
different picture. For English--Arabic, it ranges from $0.012$ to $0.644$ across individual
configurations and from $0.0578$ to $0.5667$ across groups. For English--Spanish, the weakest
configuration is slightly negative ($-0.008$), meaning that its ordering is no better than unrelated.

Standard multilingual fine-tuning therefore produces models that usually reach the same verdict in
all three languages but support it with different evidence. The top-ranked traces in English and
Arabic are nearly uncorrelated ($\tau \approx 0.13$). Because the final verdict is a majority vote
over the top five traces, stable labels can result from unstable and largely different evidence.
Verdict agreement therefore overstates cross-lingual robustness. Ranking agreement reveals the
difference. If we had reported only $\kappa$ over final answers, we would have concluded that all
systems were already language-invariant.

\subsection{Does Invariance Training Improve Ranking? (RQ1)}
\label{subsec:res-invariance}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-tradeoff} plots every configuration by mean macro-F1 (accuracy) and Kendall
$\tau$ between English and Arabic rankings (stability). The two training groups separate clearly on
stability but overlap on accuracy.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{results-tradeoff}
  \caption{Each point is one configuration ($n=28$). The vertical axis is the mean pairwise Kendall
  $\tau$ between the English and Arabic trace rankings the horizontal axis is macro-F1 averaged over
  the three languages. The invariance-trained population is fully separated from every other
  configuration on stability, while the accuracy ranges overlap.}
  \label{fig:results-tradeoff}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{The stability effect is large and fully separated.}
Table~\ref{tab:results-summary} summarises the group-level picture.
The $13$ invariance-trained configurations reach a mean English--Arabic Kendall $\tau$ of $0.5667$.
The corresponding value is $0.1308$ for standard fine-tuning, $0.0578$ for the earlier trace
scorers, and $0.2185$ for the baselines. The stability ranges do not overlap, the \emph{worst}
invariance-trained configuration ($\tau = 0.5089$) is still well above the \emph{best}
non-invariance configuration ($\tau = 0.3674$). The effect size is Cohen's $d = 6.09$
(Welch $t = 16.71$, $p < 10^{-13}$, Mann--Whitney $p < 10^{-5}$). Verdict agreement moves in the same
direction, from Fleiss $\kappa = 0.8924$ to $0.9443$ ($d = 2.20$, $p < 10^{-4}$). This complete
separation of the stability ranges is the main evidence for the effect.

\paragraph{The accuracy effect is positive but not the argument.}
Mean macro-F1 is also higher for invariance-trained models, $0.6148$ compared with $0.5977$
($d = 1.61$, $p < 0.001$). Unlike the stability ranges, however, the accuracy ranges overlap. The
best non-invariance configuration (\emph{Llama, standard}, $0.6227$) outperforms the best
invariance-trained configuration (\emph{Qwen, inv.\ $\lambda^\star$=0.1}, $0.6182$). On English
macro-F1 alone, invariance training shows no improvement, the best English score belongs to
\emph{Llama, reg.} ($0.6263$).

\paragraph{On the official ranking metric, the two are tied.}
Recall@5 needs to be considered separately because it is the official ranking metric and does
\emph{not} follow macro-F1. Averaged over the three languages, standard fine-tuning has a
marginally \emph{higher} Recall@5 than invariance training ($0.2679$ against $0.2656$), and the single
best Recall@5 of the study belongs to a standard fine-tune (\emph{Llama, reg.}, $0.2710$ in English).
Per language the two groups are within $0.007$ of each other everywhere
(Figure~\ref{fig:results-language}, right panel). Given the ceiling of $0.3005$ and the near-collinearity
of the ranking metrics noted in Section~\ref{subsec:res-task}, the fair reading is that the two
approaches are tied on Recall@5. Invariance training does not measurably change how much relevant
evidence appears in the top five.

The answer to RQ1 therefore depends on what is meant by improvement. Optimising for language
invariance does \emph{not} increase per-language Recall@5. On that metric, it matches standard
fine-tuning and is slightly lower on average. It does, however, make the trace ordering stable under
translation, improving cross-lingual ranking agreement roughly fourfold. It also improves verdict
quality and reduces the loss in Arabic. The contribution is therefore greater robustness across
languages, not higher state-of-the-art ranking performance.

\subsection{Where the Cross-Lingual Differences Live}
\label{subsec:res-language}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{results-language}
  \caption{Both official metrics by language, for the best configuration in each training group
  (selected by mean macro-F1). \emph{Top:} macro-F1. Spanish is on par with English, Arabic is
  consistently the weakest, and the Arabic gap is smallest for the invariance-trained system.
  \emph{Bottom:} Recall@5 against its attainable ceiling of $0.3005$ (dashed). The two fine-tuned
  systems are level on Recall@5 and sit close to the ceiling, so the invariance effect visible in the
  upper panel does not appear in the lower one.}
  \label{fig:results-language}
\end{figure}

Across the $28$ configurations, Spanish macro-F1 ($0.6118$) is slightly higher than English
($0.6099$). Arabic macro-F1 is $0.5951$, which is $0.0147$ below English, or about $2.4\%$ lower.
Recall@5 follows the same pattern for English, Spanish, and Arabic ($0.2579$ / $0.2620$ / $0.2548$).
The multilingual difficulty in this task is therefore mainly an Arabic effect. This is consistent
with the expectation from Section~\ref{subsec:bg-numeracy}: a different numeral script and weaker
representation in pre-training make Arabic the more difficult case~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm,Qi_2023}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{results-conflicting}
  \caption{F1 on the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class by language, for the best configuration in
  each training group (selected by mean macro-F1). The Arabic degradation seen in
  Figure~\ref{fig:results-language} is concentrated almost entirely in this class, the standard
  fine-tune loses far more of its \textsf{Conflicting} F1 between English and Arabic than the
  invariance-trained system does. Group averages are reported in the text.}
  \label{fig:results-conflicting}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-conflicting} shows that the Arabic drop is not spread evenly across labels.
Between English and Arabic, F1 falls by only $0.0026$ for \textsf{False} and $0.0027$ for
\textsf{True}. For the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class, it falls from $0.2625$ in English and
$0.2671$ in Spanish to $0.2236$ in Arabic. This drop is roughly fourteen times larger than for either
majority class. The difference becomes clearer when the models are grouped by training type.
Standard fine-tunes fall from $0.2701$ in English to $0.1781$ in Arabic on \textsf{Conflicting} F1,
a relative loss of $34.1\%$. Invariance-trained models fall only from $0.2698$ to $0.2513$, a relative
loss of $6.8\%$. Unlike the aggregate Arabic gap discussed below, this difference is statistically
clear (Welch $p = 0.0001$, Mann--Whitney $p = 0.0003$). Only two of the nine standard fine-tunes fall
within the range of the thirteen invariance-trained models. \textsf{Conflicting} is the rarest class,
with $427$ of $4{,}233$ claims ($10.1\%$). It also requires the model to weigh contradictory
quantitative evidence rather than match a single number. Unstable evidence selection is therefore
especially harmful for this class. The class-level analysis explains the aggregate result,
invariance training helps Arabic mainly by protecting the hardest and rarest decision.

\paragraph{A caution on the aggregate Arabic gap.}
The mean English-to-Arabic macro-F1 drop is $-0.0098$ for invariance-trained models and $-0.0311$
for standard fine-tuning. The difference is roughly threefold in the expected direction. However,
this comparison does \emph{not} reach statistical significance
(Welch $p = 0.089$, Mann--Whitney $p = 0.231$), because the per-configuration variance in the gap is
large relative to the sample size. The pattern is consistent across model families and settings, and
the class-level analysis supports it. Nevertheless, it should be described as a trend rather than an
established effect. The ranking-stability result, by contrast, is unambiguous.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{subsec:res-ablations}

\paragraph{The invariance weight.}
Figure~\ref{fig:results-lambda} sweeps the target invariance weight $\lambda^\star$ over
$\{0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$ for both backbones. Cross-lingual stability increases monotonically with
$\lambda^\star$ for both model families. It rises from $0.5711$ to $0.6440$ for Llama and from
$0.5820$ to $0.6426$ for Qwen. This is the expected result of increasing the regulariser strength.
Mean macro-F1 does not increase in the same way. For Llama, it peaks at $\lambda^\star = 0.2$
($0.6180$, compared with $0.6146$ at $0.1$ and $0.6116$ at $0.3$). For Qwen, it declines slightly
from $0.6182$ to $0.6173$ and then $0.6152$. Recall@5 follows macro-F1 rather than $\tau$ and peaks at
$\lambda^\star = 0.2$ for both backbones. The sweep therefore shows a mild trade-off between task
quality and consistency. The value $\lambda^\star = 0.2$ provides the best balance. All tested values
still produce much greater stability than the non-invariance systems, so using the objective matters
more than the exact weight chosen within this range.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=\linewidth]{results-lambda}
  \caption{Effect of the target invariance weight $\lambda^\star$. Cross-lingual ranking stability
  rises monotonically with $\lambda^\star$ for both backbones, whereas both task metrics macro-F1
  and Recall@5 peak at $\lambda^\star = 0.2$. The objective therefore trades a little task quality
  for consistency beyond that point, making $\lambda^\star = 0.2$ the balanced choice.}
  \label{fig:results-lambda}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Numeric normalisation does not combine with invariance training.}
Plain-text numeric normalisation was carried over from the shared-task system as a complementary method
for the numerical dimension of the task (Section~\ref{subsec:method-numeric}). Within the
invariance-trained group it does not help. Configurations combining the two reach $\tau = 0.5121$ and
mean macro-F1 $0.6133$, against $\tau = 0.5910$ and $0.6154$ for invariance training alone, adding
numeric normalisation reduces Kendall $\tau$ by $0.079$ and does not improve accuracy. The same
pattern appears in Arabic ($0.6028$ compared with $0.6111$). Outside the invariance group, the result
is different, numeric normalisation gives a small improvement to standard fine-tunes in English and
Spanish. The two methods therefore seem partly redundant rather than individually ineffective. Both
address the fact that the same quantity can be represented differently across scripts. The invariance
objective already forces scores for these forms to agree, so adding canonical values to the input may
increase its length without providing new information. The evidence does not support combining the
two methods.

\paragraph{Backbone families.}
Across all settings, Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B have similar accuracy, their mean macro-F1 scores are
$0.6128$ and $0.6083$, respectively. Qwen is more stable across languages, with $\tau = 0.4505$
compared with $0.3161$ and Fleiss $\kappa = 0.9353$ compared with $0.9195$. Mistral-7B is weaker on
both dimensions ($0.5843$ and $0.1197$), supporting the decision to keep it as a reference rather
than extend it with the invariance objective. No single configuration is best on every measure.
\emph{Llama, reg.} has the best English macro-F1 ($0.6263$) and Recall@5 ($0.2710$).
\emph{Llama, inv.\ $\lambda^\star$=0.2} has the best Arabic macro-F1 ($0.6161$), while
\emph{Llama, inv.\ $\lambda^\star$=0.3} has the best ranking stability ($\tau = 0.6440$). This split
again shows why accuracy and stability must be considered separately.

\subsection{The Relationship Between Invariance and Reasoning Quality (RQ2)}
\label{subsec:res-relationship}

For RQ2, we examine language invariance and reasoning quality as separate properties. This lets us
study their relationship directly. Here, \emph{mean macro-F1} represents
reasoning quality. It is the claim-verdict macro-F1 averaged across the three languages. We report
Recall@5 separately because verdict quality and ranking quality behave differently.

Over the $28$ configurations, mean macro-F1 correlates with Kendall $\tau$ at $r = 0.60$ and with
Fleiss $\kappa$ at $r = 0.57$. The corresponding correlations for the ranking metric are weaker,
mean Recall@5 correlates with $\tau$ at only $r = 0.39$ (English Recall@5 alone, $r = 0.49$), while
its correlation with $\kappa$ is $r = 0.57$. Quality and consistency therefore have a positive but
moderate relationship, mainly for verdict quality. The amount of relevant evidence in the top five
says relatively little about whether the model retrieves the same evidence in another language.

The relationship is not deterministic. Figure~\ref{fig:results-tradeoff} shows that configurations
with nearly identical mean macro-F1 ($0.61$--$0.62$) span $\tau$ values from $0.08$ to $0.64$. Thus,
at almost fixed verdict quality, stability varies by more than $0.5$. Mean macro-F1 is therefore a
weak predictor of stability, and the two properties are separate in practice as well as in principle.
This confirms in a ranking setting what prior work found for factual
prediction~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023}, a more accurate
multilingual model is not necessarily more consistent, and improving task quality alone does not
ensure consistency.

Fleiss $\kappa$ and Kendall $\tau$ correlate at $r = 0.83$ across configurations, so verdict and
ranking consistency are related. However, Section~\ref{subsec:res-consistency} showed that $\kappa$
is compressed into a narrow high range while $\tau$ varies widely. The measures are therefore not
interchangeable, despite their high correlation.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:res-threats}

\paragraph{Context-budget confound.}
The most important limitation concerns the training context. The invariance-trained models used a
smaller context budget because aligned trilingual batching multiplies the number of sequences in each
optimisation step. The available compute did not support the full long-context setting. Several
standard fine-tunes used the full context budget. Training method and context budget are therefore
partly entangled, so this is not a fully controlled comparison.

Two observations reduce the concern. First, the confound works against the invariance-trained models,
which use the smaller context budget. Their gains are therefore conservative estimates. Second, we
can estimate the context effect from architectures tested at both budgets. Moving from the reduced to
the full budget changes macro-F1 by $+0.0052$ in English, $+0.0043$ in Spanish, and $-0.0053$ in
Arabic. The mean change is only $+0.0014$, which is smaller than the invariance effect and does not
even have the same direction in every language. Under the matched reduced-budget comparison,
invariance-trained models still improve mean macro-F1 by $+0.0083$, Arabic macro-F1 by $+0.0197$,
and Kendall $\tau$ by a factor of $4.85$. No invariance-trained configuration was tested with the
full context budget. That experiment would resolve the confound and is the most important next step.
The evidence therefore supports the following guarded claim,
\emph{invariance training at a reduced context budget matches or exceeds full-context fine-tuning on
mean macro-F1, and dominates it on cross-lingual stability}. The stability result is
unlikely to be explained by this confound because the estimated context effect is far too small to
produce a fourfold change in $\tau$.

\paragraph{Small comparison arms.}
The matched-budget comparison contains only two short-context fine-tunes, one Llama and one Qwen. The
full-budget group contains seven configurations. The group means are therefore imprecise. Spanish is
also a clear exception to the general pattern, \emph{Llama, short context} reaches $0.6220$ Spanish
macro-F1, above all $13$ invariance-trained configurations. We therefore do not claim an improvement
for Spanish. The supported conclusion is parity in English and Spanish, an improvement in Arabic,
and a large improvement in cross-lingual stability.

\paragraph{Behavioural, not representational, evidence.}
All invariance measures in this study are behavioural, they compare model outputs across languages.
As noted in Section~\ref{subsec:bg-multilingual}, matching outputs do not prove that the model stores or
processes the underlying knowledge in the same way~\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}.
The results show that the objective changes what the model \emph{does} across languages, but not what
changes inside the model.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{subsec:res-summary}

\begin{description}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Does optimising for language-invariant reasoning improve ranking
performance?} Not on the official ranking metric. Mean
Recall@5 is $0.2656$ for invariance training against $0.2679$ for standard fine-tuning, and the best
Recall@5 in the study ($0.2710$) belongs to a standard fine-tune. What it does produce is a fourfold,
fully separated improvement in the cross-lingual stability of the ranking
($\tau = 0.567$ compared with $0.131$, $d = 6.09$). It also produces a small but consistent gain in
verdict quality ($0.6148$ compared with $0.5977$ mean macro-F1). Per-language ranking quality remains
essentially unchanged, but ranking \emph{consistency} across languages improves substantially.

\item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{What is the relationship between language invariance and reasoning
quality?} The relationship is positive but moderate. Mean macro-F1, which represents verdict quality across the
three languages, correlates with ranking stability at $r=0.60$ and with verdict agreement at $r=0.57$.
Mean Recall@5 is a weaker predictor, correlating with $\tau$ at only $r=0.39$. Even at almost fixed
mean macro-F1, stability varies by more than $0.5$ in $\tau$. Consistency is therefore not a
by-product of task quality, and the two must be measured separately. The random baseline reinforces
this conclusion because it is perfectly invariant but uninformative.

\item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Can language-invariant training objectives lead to more robust
multilingual reasoning models?} Yes, with the qualifications above. The gains are concentrated where
robustness is most needed,
in Arabic, which uses a different script and is the least well-represented of the three languages. In
Arabic, the gain is concentrated in the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class, where standard
fine-tuning degrades several times more than invariance training. The effect appears with both Llama
and Qwen, suggesting that it comes from the method rather than one model family. It is also achieved
with a reduced training-context budget.
\end{description}

One observation connects all three questions. Standard multilingual fine-tuning already produces
models that usually agree on the verdict across languages. It does not produce models that agree on
\emph{why}. The invariance objective mainly aligns the evidence, not the final label. Verdict-level
