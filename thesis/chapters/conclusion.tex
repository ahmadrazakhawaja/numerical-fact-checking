\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis asked whether language invariance is a useful \emph{objective} for multilingual reasoning,
rather than only a property worth measuring. We studied multilingual numerical claim verification in
CLEF~CheckThat!~2026 Task~2~\cite{clef-checkthat:2026:task2}. Given a numerical claim, its evidence,
and a set of candidate reasoning traces, a system must rank the traces and derive a claim-level
verdict in English, Spanish, and Arabic. We began with the supervised trace scorer from our
shared-task submission~\cite{khawaja2026gipplab}. We then added a score-level language-invariance
objective that penalises different relevance profiles for corresponding traces across languages. We
also introduced an evaluation protocol that separates cross-lingual consistency from task accuracy.
The study covers $28$ valid configurations, three open-weight backbones, and a held-out trilingual
test set of $4{,}233$ parallel claims. The results show that invariance is useful, but mainly for
cross-lingual robustness rather than per-language ranking quality.

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{subsec:concl-rqs}

\paragraph{RQ1: Does optimising for language-invariant reasoning improve ranking performance?}
Not on the official ranking metric, but it makes the ranking much more stable across languages.
Invariance training and standard multilingual fine-tuning have indistinguishable Recall@5 scores.
The best Recall@5 belongs to a standard fine-tune, and both approaches reach roughly $90\%$ of the
metric's attainable ceiling. However, English--Arabic rank agreement increases roughly fourfold. The
stability ranges of the invariance-trained and non-invariance systems do not overlap. Verdict quality
also improves modestly. The supported claim is therefore about cross-lingual robustness, not higher
ranking accuracy.

The ranking component of this task is already close to saturated. For $13.7\%$ of claims, none of the
candidate traces matches the gold verdict. Better ranking cannot improve Recall@5 for those claims.
Further gains will require generating or retrieving better candidate traces. Given this ceiling, an
objective that preserves retrieval quality while making rankings reproducible across languages
improves a dimension that still has substantial room for progress.

\paragraph{RQ2: What is the relationship between language invariance and reasoning quality?}
The relationship is positive and moderate, but neither property can replace the other. Verdict
quality correlates with cross-lingual rank agreement at $r = 0.60$. Yet even at almost fixed verdict
quality, the observed agreement spans more than half the range of Kendall's $\tau$. The ranking metric
is an even weaker predictor of consistency. Two findings make this distinction clear. First, the
random-ranking baseline is perfectly
language-invariant by construction but performs poorly. Consistency alone therefore does not show
quality. Second, every evaluated system, including the weakest, has high verdict agreement across
languages, while rank agreement varies by almost an order of magnitude. Models often reach the same
verdict in all three languages using largely different evidence.

This difference has an important methodological consequence. If we had measured cross-lingual
consistency only from final answers, we would have concluded that all systems were already
language-invariant. The main effect would have remained invisible. Agreement on discrete outputs is
useful but insufficient. When a system produces an intermediate structure, such as a trace ranking,
agreement should also be measured at that level. This extends the known separation between accuracy
and consistency from factual prediction to a ranking
setting~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023}.

\paragraph{RQ3: Can language-invariant training objectives lead to more robust multilingual reasoning
models?} Yes. The gains appear where the multilingual task is most difficult. Spanish behaves almost
the same as English for every system, so the main cross-lingual challenge is Arabic. This is consistent
with the expectation that Arabic's different numeral script and weaker representation in pre-training
make it harder~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm}. Within Arabic, the largest drop occurs in the
minority \textsf{Conflicting} class. This label requires the model to weigh contradictory quantitative
evidence rather than match a single number. Standard fine-tuning degrades sharply on this class, while
invariance training largely maintains performance. The effect appears with both Llama and Qwen,
suggesting that it comes from the objective rather than one model family. It was also achieved with a
\emph{reduced} training-context budget, making the comparison conservative.

The value-aware numerical components from the shared-task system did not add to this gain. Combining
plain-text numeric normalisation with the invariance objective reduced cross-lingual stability and did
not improve accuracy. Numeric normalisation still gave a small benefit to standard fine-tunes. The
most likely explanation is redundancy rather than general harm. Both methods address differences in
how the same quantity is represented across scripts. Once the score-level constraint is active,
adding canonical values to the input may increase its length without adding new information. The
evidence therefore does not support combining the two methods.

\subsection{Contributions Revisited}
\label{subsec:concl-contrib}

This thesis delivers the contributions listed in Section~\ref{sec:contributions}. First, it defines
language invariance as a differentiable training objective for a competitive trace-scoring pipeline.
The objective works on continuous per-trace scores, which can be aligned directly across languages.
Generative rankers produce only discrete orderings that must first be reconciled. Second, the thesis
compares ranking formulations from the perspective of consistency. Prompted listwise, pairwise, and
preference-based ranking remain sensitive to candidate order, while supervised trace scoring avoids
this dependence. Third, it tests value-aware numerical representations together with the invariance
objective and reports the negative interaction described above. Finally, it introduces an evaluation
protocol that reports task quality and cross-lingual agreement separately at both the verdict and
ranking levels. The results show why ranking-level agreement is the more informative measure here.

Two additional findings were not anticipated. First, Recall@5, Precision@5, MRR@5, macro-F1, and the
majority-class F1 scores are nearly collinear across configurations. These metrics largely produce the
same ordering of systems, while the minority \textsf{Conflicting} class separates them. Second, a
seeded random ranker is a useful diagnostic baseline. It is invariant by construction and therefore
provides a clear example of why consistency and quality are independent.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:concl-limits}

Section~\ref{subsec:res-threats} discusses the threats to validity in full. Two limitations are
especially important. First, the invariance-trained models used a smaller context budget than several
standard fine-tunes, so training method and context budget are partly entangled. This confound is
conservative because the invariance-trained group has the smaller budget. The measured effect of
extra context is also small and changes direction across languages. Even so, this is an observational
comparison rather than a controlled ablation. Second, all invariance measures are behavioural. The
results show that the objective changes what a model \emph{does} across languages, but not how it
represents the content internally. Matching behaviour does not necessarily imply a shared internal
representation~\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}. The improvement is also not
uniform. The best short-context standard fine-tune has higher Spanish macro-F1 than every
invariance-trained configuration, and the aggregate Arabic gap does not reach statistical
significance on its own, although its direction is consistent.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:concl-future}

Several directions follow from these results. The most valuable next experiment is also the least
expensive: run one invariance-trained configuration with the full context budget. A single seed would
provide an initial check and help resolve the context-budget confound. The language set should also
be extended to more scripts and language families to test whether the effect generalises beyond
Arabic.

A second direction is to study the mechanism. Probing or representational-similarity analysis of the
trace scorer's hidden states~\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent,STOLLE2026100250} could test
whether score-level alignment also brings the internal representations of parallel inputs closer.
Alternatively, the objective may only constrain the output layer. This distinction would show whether
invariance acts as an inductive bias or only as a surface constraint.

A third direction is to address the ranking ceiling. Ranking is nearly saturated on the current
candidate set, so future work should generate reasoning traces that cover the gold verdict more often.
For the $13.7\%$ of claims with no gold-matching trace, no verifier can improve Recall@5. The
score-level invariance objective is also not limited to fact-checking. It can be used wherever a model
assigns comparable scores to parallel items. Testing it on other multilingual ranking and reranking
tasks would show how widely the result generalises.

\subsection{Closing Remarks}
\label{subsec:concl-closing}

The main finding depends on separating two ideas. Standard multilingual fine-tuning already produces
models that usually reach the same verdict across languages. It does not ensure that they reach that
verdict for the same reasons. Training for language invariance does not improve the model's ability to
find relevant evidence, and it does not measurably change the official ranking metric. Instead, it
makes the evidence used by the model more consistent across languages. A claim and its translation
are therefore not only assigned the same label; they are more often assessed using the same evidence.
In fact-checking, the justification matters as much as the verdict. This property is therefore
valuable, but it remains invisible when evaluation measures agreement only on final answers.
