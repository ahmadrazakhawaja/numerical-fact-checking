\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This section reviews work on LLM-based ranking, multilingual fact-checking,
cross-lingual consistency, numerical reasoning, and preference-based fine-tuning.
Together, these areas frame the central question of the thesis, if a model is
trained to behave consistently across languages, do its ranking and reasoning
also become more reliable?

The review follows the three research questions. RQ1 concerns ranking performance,
RQ2 concerns the relationship between invariance and reasoning quality across
English, Spanish, and Arabic, and RQ3 concerns multilingual robustness. We first
consider LLMs as rankers and judges, including their presentation biases. We then
review multilingual fact-checking and cross-lingual consistency. The final parts
cover multilingual representations, numerical reasoning, preference optimisation,
and parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

\subsection{LLMs as Rankers and Rerankers}
\label{subsec:ranking}

LLMs are often used as rankers without task-specific training, especially in
retrieval and evaluation. In pairwise ranking, the model compares two candidates
at a time, and the comparisons are combined into a final ordering. Each decision
is relatively simple, but the number of comparisons grows quadratically with the
number of candidates~\cite{qin2024largelanguagemodelseffective}.

Listwise ranking presents all candidates at once and asks the model for a complete
ordering. It requires fewer inference calls, but its output depends on the entire
prompt, including the order in which candidates appear. Recent work adapts
learning-to-rank objectives to preference optimisation for more direct listwise
training~\cite{liu-etal-2025-lipo}. Even so, generative rankers remain sensitive
to candidate order and prompt format, rather than only to candidate
content~\cite{qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect}.

This limitation motivates the independently scored design used in this thesis.
The model scores each reasoning trace separately, and these scores determine the
final ranking. The ranking can therefore reflect trace quality without depending
on artefacts of a multi-candidate prompt.

\subsection{Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation}
\label{subsec:judge-bias}

Similar problems occur when LLMs act as evaluators. Their judgments can depend on
how options are presented, not only on content quality. Position bias makes a
candidate more or less likely to be selected because of its location in the
prompt. Verbosity bias can favour longer answers~\cite{shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy}.
These biases can change the selected candidate even when its content is unchanged.

The main concern here is that LLM judges are often not language-invariant. The
same evaluation can produce different judgments in different languages. A recent
study of five model families, five tasks, and twenty-five languages found low
multilingual judge agreement, with an average Fleiss' $\kappa$ of roughly $0.3$.
Neither multilingual pre-training nor larger model scale reliably removed the
inconsistency~\cite{fu2025reliablemultilingualllmasajudge}. This is the kind of
cross-lingual instability studied in this thesis.

\subsection{Automated and Multilingual Fact-Checking}
\label{subsec:factcheck}

Automated fact-checking is increasingly treated as an LLM reasoning and assessment
problem. Recent work examines how task formulation affects the reliability of
real-world claim verification, rather than asking only whether a model can produce
a label~\cite{sahitaj2025automatedfactcheckingrealworldclaims}. Numerical claims
are especially difficult because a small change in scope, units, dates, or
interpretation can change the correct verdict.

QuanTemp pairs real-world, open-domain numerical claims with supporting
evidence~\cite{v2024quantemprealworldopendomainbenchmark}. Later work extends this
setting by generating several reasoning traces for each claim and using a verifier
to select the most useful ones~\cite{chungkham2025thinkrightmoretesttime}.
CLEF~CheckThat!~2026 Task~2 adopts this verifier-guided setup for English,
Spanish, and Arabic. Systems rank candidate traces and derive a claim-level
verdict~\cite{clef-checkthat:2026:task2}.

Broader multilingual benchmarks show the same difficulty. X-Fact contains
naturally occurring, expert-labelled claims in twenty-five languages from eleven
language families. Even strong models achieve only modest accuracy, showing that
fact-checking ability transfers poorly across languages
\cite{gupta2021xfactnewbenchmarkdataset}. XFEVER translates FEVER claims and
evidence into several languages to test zero-shot and translate-train verification.
Performance varies substantially by language and generally remains below English
\cite{chang2023xfeverexploringfactverification}. X-FACTR reaches a similar
conclusion for factual recall. Its native-speaker-authored queries in twenty-three
languages reveal clear cross-lingual differences
\cite{jiang2020xfactrmultilingualfactualknowledge}.

Together, these studies show why multilingual fact-checking is both important and
difficult. They also provide the practical motivation for this thesis, if
fact-checking systems behave differently across languages, then language
invariance becomes a useful objective to study directly.

\subsection{Language Invariance and Cross-Lingual Consistency}
\label{subsec:invariance}

Language invariance requires a model to give consistent judgments when the same
claim is expressed in different languages. This is central to a task that presents
equivalent numerical claims in English, Spanish, and Arabic. To study it
experimentally, the form of consistency must be defined and measured separately
from accuracy.

\paragraph{Defining consistency.}
Consistency is commonly defined as stability under meaning-preserving changes to
the input. A model is consistent if quasi-paraphrases of the same query produce
non-contradictory predictions. The consistency score is the proportion of
paraphrase pairs with agreeing predictions
\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained}. This definition separates
consistency from accuracy. A model can be consistently wrong or accurate on
average while giving inconsistent answers to equivalent inputs.

The same definition extends to translations. A language-invariant model should
give the same output when two versions of a claim have the same meaning. However,
mBERT and XLM-R are no more consistent across languages than a monolingual model
is across English paraphrases. Consistency also decreases when typologically
distant languages are included~\cite{Fierro_2022}. We therefore use
\emph{language invariance} to mean agreement across translated versions of the
same input, measured independently of correctness.

\paragraph{Measuring cross-lingual consistency.}
Cross-lingual consistency therefore needs its own evaluation. Multilingual models
often have low factual consistency across languages, and increasing model size
does not reliably solve the problem. Consistency is also strongly related to
subword-vocabulary overlap~\cite{Qi_2023}. Script and tokenisation can therefore
affect cross-lingual behaviour independently of reasoning ability.

The appropriate agreement measure depends on the output. For categorical verdicts,
Cohen's $\kappa$ measures agreement between two raters
\cite{cohen1960coefficient}, Fleiss' $\kappa$ extends it to more than two
raters~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}, and Krippendorff's $\alpha$ can also handle
missing data~\cite{krippendorff2004content}. The multilingual LLM-as-a-judge work
discussed above also uses Fleiss' $\kappa$
\cite{fu2025reliablemultilingualllmasajudge}. For rankings, Kendall's $\tau$ and
Spearman's $\rho$ measure rank correlation~\cite{kendall1938new,spearman1904proof},
while Jaccard similarity measures overlap between top-$k$ sets.

Behavioural agreement does not prove that a model uses the same internal
representation across languages. Models can answer consistently while storing
knowledge separately, especially across different scripts
\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}. This thesis therefore treats
invariance as an observable behavioural property, not as proof of a shared
multilingual reasoning mechanism.

\paragraph{A measurement framing for this thesis.}
We measure language invariance at three levels. First, our primary evaluation is
\emph{verdict invariance}, which asks whether the predicted claim label remains stable across languages. For a
claim $c$ in
$\mathcal{L} = \{\text{English}, \text{Spanish}, \text{Arabic}\}$, let
$\hat{y}_\ell(c)$ be the verdict predicted from its language-$\ell$ version. We
report the proportion of aligned claims for which all predictions agree. We also
compute Fleiss' $\kappa$ over
$\{\hat{y}_\ell(c)\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}}$, treating the three languages as
raters of the same claim~\cite{fleiss1971measuring}. Pairwise Cohen's
$\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} and Krippendorff's
$\alpha$~\cite{krippendorff2004content} provide supporting diagnostics.

As a secondary diagnostic, \emph{ranking invariance} compares the trace orderings themselves. Let
$r_\ell(c)$ denote the ordering produced for claim $c$ in language $\ell$. We
compare these orderings with mean pairwise Kendall's $\tau$, Spearman's
$\rho$~\cite{kendall1938new,spearman1904proof}, and top-$k$ Jaccard overlap. These
metrics show whether stability exists in the reasoning-trace ranking or only in
the final verdict.

Third, training encourages \emph{score-level invariance}. The model is penalised
when it assigns different normalised score vectors to aligned traces across
languages. This regulariser is related to the consistency objectives discussed in
Section~\ref{subsec:peft} Section~\ref{sec:methodology} gives the exact
formulation. All invariance measures remain separate from task accuracy. This is
essential for RQ2 because a model may be consistent but wrong, or accurate but
unstable~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained, Qi_2023}.

\subsection{Cross-Lingual Representations and the Latent Language}
\label{subsec:representations}

Language invariance is plausible only if multilingual models share some
representation of meaning. Evidence suggests that they do. mBERT transfers in
zero-shot settings even across scripts, although typological similarity affects
the result~\cite{pires2019multilingualmultilingualbert}. Shared multilingual
structure can also emerge without a shared
vocabulary~\cite{wu2020emergingcrosslingualstructurepretrained}. XLM-R shows that
large-scale multilingual pre-training improves transfer across many
languages~\cite{conneau2020unsupervisedcrosslingualrepresentationlearning}, while
explicit alignment can bring contextual representations closer
together~\cite{cao2020multilingualalignmentcontextualword}. A recent survey reviews
these alignment methods and their evaluation~\cite{hämmerl2024understandingcrosslingualalignment}.

Decoder-only LLMs show a more complex pattern. Logit-lens studies suggest that
models such as Llama process multilingual input through intermediate
representations that are often closest to English before producing the target
language. This has been described as a \emph{latent language}
\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent}. Other work also places semantic
decisions in a representation space close to English and finds interventions most
effective there~\cite{schut2025multilingualllmsthinkenglish}. Representational
similarity analyses suggest that reasoning is only partly aligned across
languages~\cite{STOLLE2026100250}.

These findings suggest that invariance training could encourage more consistent
use of a partly shared semantic space. It may therefore act as an inductive bias
for multilingual robustness (RQ3). However, equal answers do not necessarily
imply equal internal representations
\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}. Behavioural invariance is
evidence of stability, not proof of a language-independent reasoning mechanism.

\subsection{Numerical Reasoning Across Languages and Value-Aware Representations}
\label{subsec:numeric}

Numerical reasoning is a known weakness of LLMs. Models can fail even on simple
operations, a pattern described as a fragile number
sense~\cite{rahman2025fragilenumbersenseprobing}. Smaller diagnostic tests reach
the same conclusion~\cite{rahman2025largelanguagemodelsnumberland}. NumGLUE and
broader quantitative-reasoning evaluations show that numerical tasks test whether
a model understands quantities rather than only their surrounding
text~\cite{mishra2022numgluesuitefundamentalchallenging,lewkowycz2022solvingquantitativereasoningproblems}.

Standard token representations do not directly encode numerical magnitude. The
model sees a number as a token sequence and must infer its value from the surface
form. Some embeddings capture magnitude surprisingly well, but subword
tokenisation still limits exact numeracy~\cite{wallace2019nlpmodelsknownumbers}.
Value-aware methods instead encode the value of a numeric span
\cite{dutulescu2026valueawarenumericalrepresentationstransformer}, while related
methods expose canonical numerical information directly
\cite{schwartz2024numerologicnumberencodingenhanced}.

The problem becomes more important across languages. MGSM and other multilingual
chain-of-thought evaluations show that quantitative reasoning quality changes with
the language even when the arithmetic remains the
same~\cite{shi2022languagemodelsmultilingualchainofthought}. This matters directly
for numerical claims in English, Spanish, and Arabic.

Changing only the numeral script or format can substantially reduce accuracy for
underrepresented scripts. Tokenisation partly explains the effect, while targeted
prompting and explicit numeral mapping can reduce it
\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm}. Models also struggle with the
compositional structure of numerals across languages unless the relevant
operations are made symbolically explicit
\cite{bhattacharya2025investigatinginteractionlinguisticmathematical}.

Numeral script is therefore another source of cross-lingual variation. Numerical
fact-checking provides a demanding test of invariance because the same value can
appear in different scripts. Value-aware representations and numerical
normalisation may improve both numerical accuracy and cross-lingual consistency,
especially for Arabic.

\subsection{Preference Optimisation and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}
\label{subsec:peft}

The training setup builds on preference optimisation and parameter-efficient
fine-tuning. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) increases the likelihood of a
preferred output relative to a rejected one while keeping the model close to a
reference policy. It therefore learns from pairwise preferences without a separate
reward model or reinforcement learning
\cite{rafailov2024directpreferenceoptimizationlanguage}.

Parameter-efficient methods make these experiments feasible under a modest GPU
budget. LoRA freezes the base model and trains only small low-rank update
matrices~\cite{hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge}. QLoRA further reduces memory use
by loading the base model at low precision and training adapters on top of
it~\cite{dettmers2023qloraefficientfinetuningquantized}. These methods support the
open-weight instruction-tuned models used here, Mistral-7B
\cite{jiang2023mistral7b}, Qwen3-8B~\cite{qwen3technicalreport}, and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct~\cite{llama31instruct}.

Recent objectives apply similar ideas to cross-lingual consistency. Zheng et
al.~\cite{zheng2021consistencyregularizationcrosslingualfinetuning} use
consistency regularisation to penalise prediction changes under
meaning-preserving perturbations, including code-switching and machine translation.
Cross-lingual self-alignment samples answers in several languages, selects the most
self-consistent answer as the preferred target, and applies DPO
\cite{wang2025calmunleashingcrosslingualselfaligning}. Direct Consistency
Optimization aligns completion likelihoods across parallel prompts in different
languages without an explicit reward model
\cite{liu2026posttraininglanguagemodelscrosslingual}.

These methods show that preference optimisation can target consistency as well as
general output quality. Most are evaluated on factual question answering or broad
benchmarks, however. They do not test whether optimising for cross-lingual
consistency improves ranking in numerical fact-checking. RQ1 addresses this gap.

\subsection{Positioning of This Thesis}
\label{subsec:positioning}

The literature reveals a clear gap. LLM rankers and judges are sensitive to
candidate order and prompt format
\cite{shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy,qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect}.
They can also produce different verdicts or rankings for equivalent inputs in
different languages. Larger models and multilingual pre-training do not reliably
solve this problem~\cite{fu2025reliablemultilingualllmasajudge,Qi_2023,Fierro_2022}.
At the same time, multilingual models learn partly shared, often English-centred
representations~\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent,
conneau2020unsupervisedcrosslingualrepresentationlearning}. Language-invariant
behaviour may therefore be achievable, although it would not by itself prove a
shared internal representation~\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}.

The numerical setting adds another difficulty. Standard token representations
capture numerical meaning only partly, and numerical reasoning remains
fragile~\cite{wallace2019nlpmodelsknownumbers,
rahman2025fragilenumbersenseprobing}. Performance also depends on numeral script
and format~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm}. The model must therefore rank
traces for quantities expressed in different linguistic and script forms. Recent
work shows that preference optimisation and consistency regularisation can target
cross-lingual consistency rather than only output quality
\cite{liu2026posttraininglanguagemodelscrosslingual,
wang2025calmunleashingcrosslingualselfaligning,
zheng2021consistencyregularizationcrosslingualfinetuning}. What remains unknown is
whether this improves multilingual reasoning in a trace-ranking fact-checking
task.

This thesis studies that question in CLEF~CheckThat!~2026 Task~2. Systems rank
reasoning traces and produce a verdict for numerical claims in English, Spanish,
and Arabic. The research questions separate ranking performance, the relationship
between invariance and quality, and broader multilingual robustness.

\paragraph{RQ1: Ranking performance.}
Prior work shows that consistency can be optimised directly
\cite{zheng2021consistencyregularizationcrosslingualfinetuning,
wang2025calmunleashingcrosslingualselfaligning,
liu2026posttraininglanguagemodelscrosslingual}, but most of this work evaluates
factual question answering or general benchmarks. RQ1 asks whether adding a
language-invariance objective to the trace scorer improves the official ranking
metrics, rather than only making outputs more similar across languages. This
connects to work on ranking bias
\cite{shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy,
qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect,
qin2024largelanguagemodelseffective}, if rankings are affected by irrelevant
input variation, reducing language-driven variation may make them more reliable.

\paragraph{RQ2: Invariance and reasoning quality.}
Consistency is not the same as accuracy~\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,
Qi_2023}. A model can be stable across languages but wrong, or accurate in one
language while unstable across others. RQ2 measures invariance and per-language
reasoning quality separately and then examines their relationship across English,
Spanish, and Arabic. Vocabulary overlap and script may affect consistency
\cite{Qi_2023}, and agreement need not imply shared internal representations
\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}. The relationship between
invariance and quality is therefore an empirical question.

\paragraph{RQ3: Multilingual robustness.}
The evidence reviewed above suggests that multilingual models share part of their
semantic space
\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent, schut2025multilingualllmsthinkenglish,
conneau2020unsupervisedcrosslingualrepresentationlearning}. RQ3 asks whether an
invariance objective can use this structure as an inductive bias for robust
multilingual reasoning. The experiments cover English, Spanish, and Arabic, with
particular attention to the additional script and tokenisation variation in
Arabic~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm}. Value-aware representations and
number-encoding methods provide complementary ways to reduce variation in the
surface form of quantities
\cite{dutulescu2026valueawarenumericalrepresentationstransformer,
schwartz2024numerologicnumberencodingenhanced}. With this research gap established,
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the trace scorer, the language-invariance objective,
and the experimental design used to address these questions.
