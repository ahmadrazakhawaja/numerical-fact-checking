\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes how we test language invariance as a training objective for multilingual
reasoning. We begin with the supervised trace-ranking system from our CLEF~CheckThat!~2026 Task~2
submission~\cite{khawaja2026gipplab,clef-checkthat:2026:task2}. That system already contains two
value-aware numerical components: numeric embeddings and plain-text numerical normalisation. Both aim
to reduce variation in numeral script and format, especially for Arabic.

We extend the system in two ways. First, a score-level invariance objective encourages similar scores
for corresponding traces across languages. This tests whether invariance improves ranking performance
and robustness (RQ1 and RQ3). Second, the evaluation protocol measures invariance separately from task
quality. Their relationship can therefore be observed rather than assumed (RQ2). We also test whether
the inherited numerical components complement the invariance objective (RQ3).

The chapter proceeds from the task and data to the trace scorer and its classification loss. It then
defines the invariance objective, numerical components, baselines, evaluation protocol, and experiment
matrix for Mistral-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Qwen3-8B.

\subsection{Task Formulation}
\label{subsec:method-task}

We use the verifier-guided reranking formulation of CLEF~CheckThat!~2026
Task~2~\cite{clef-checkthat:2026:task2}, which follows test-time scaling for numerical claim
verification~\cite{chungkham2025thinkrightmoretesttime,v2024quantemprealworldopendomainbenchmark}.
Each example contains a claim $c$, retrieved evidence $E(c)$, and candidate reasoning traces
$R(c)=\{1,\dots,n_c\}$. The traces were generated at several decoding temperatures to produce
different reasoning paths. Each trace $i$ has a verdict $v_i$, while the claim has a gold label
$y^\star_c$ from
$\mathcal{Y}=\{\textsf{True},\textsf{False},\textsf{Conflicting}\}$. The system outputs a trace
ranking $r(c)$ and a claim verdict $\hat{y}_c\in\mathcal{Y}$.

The official evaluation is performed independently for each language
$\ell\in\mathcal{L}=\{\text{English},\text{Spanish},\text{Arabic}\}$. Ranking quality is measured by
Recall@5 over the traces whose verdict matches the gold claim label,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recall5}
\mathrm{Recall@5}(c)=
\frac{\bigl|\{\,i\in r_{1:5}(c)\;:\;v_i=y^\star_c\,\}\bigr|}
     {\bigl|\{\,i\in R(c)\;:\;v_i=y^\star_c\,\}\bigr|},
\end{equation}
where $r_{1:5}(c)$ denotes the top five traces. Macro-F1 measures claim-verdict quality, and the
official per-language score is the mean of macro-F1 and Recall@5. We also report MRR@5. Recall@5 has
a structural ceiling. With $m$ gold-matching traces, even a perfect ranking achieves at most
$\min(5,m)/m$, when $m=0$, Recall@5 is zero. On the validation data, the maximum attainable values are
$0.39$, $0.32$, and $0.28$ for English, Spanish, and Arabic. These ceilings limit the headroom of
every ranker.

Generative rankers, listwise prompting, pairwise prompting, and DPO over complete rankings remain
sensitive to candidate order and prompt format
\cite{qin2024largelanguagemodelseffective,shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy,rafailov2024directpreferenceoptimizationlanguage,qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect,fu2025reliablemultilingualllmasajudge}.
Supervised trace scoring instead assigns an independent relevance score to each claim--trace pair and
sorts these scores deterministically. We use this scorer for the invariance objective because its
continuous scores can be aligned directly across languages. A generative ranker would first require
reconciling discrete orderings.

\subsection{Data}
\label{subsec:method-data}

We use the shared task's multilingual numerical claim-verification dataset. The English portion is
based on QuanTemp~\cite{v2024quantemprealworldopendomainbenchmark}. The Spanish and Arabic portions
come from the previous edition and contain $2{,}808$ and $3{,}260$ claims, respectively, together with
their evidence collections~\cite{clef-checkthat:2026:task2}. Each example includes a claim, evidence
snippets, reasoning traces (\texttt{Reasoning\_traces}), per-trace verdicts
(\texttt{Verdict\_list}), and a gold label (\texttt{label}). We map both verdict types to the common
label space from Section~\ref{subsec:method-task}. A trace is relevant when its normalised verdict
matches the normalised gold label.

\paragraph{Complete and positive-only splits.}
Each training split has a \emph{complete} and a \emph{positive-only} version. The complete split keeps
all claims. The positive-only split removes claims for which no trace has the correct verdict. Such a
claim provides only negative examples to the binary scorer and increases class imbalance without
teaching the distinction between useful and incorrect reasoning. Filtering is therefore applied only
during training. Evaluation always uses the complete split, including claims for which Recall@5 must
be zero.

\paragraph{Aligned multilingual data for invariance.}
The invariance objective requires parallel examples. We create translated versions of each claim,
its evidence, and its traces. The original \texttt{dataset\_index} and trace ordering are preserved,
so trace $i$ corresponds to trace $i$ for the same claim in every language. This alignment allows
their scores to be compared directly.

\subsection{Trace-Scoring Architecture}
\label{subsec:method-scorer}

\paragraph{Backbone models.}
The trace scorer uses three open-weight, instruction-tuned decoder models:
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\cite{jiang2023mistral7b}, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct~\cite{llama31instruct},
and Qwen3-8B~\cite{qwen3technicalreport}. Each backbone is loaded as a sequence-classification
model. The pretrained transformer processes the claim--trace input, and a lightweight linear head
maps its final representation to a relevance score.

The three families differ in their pre-training data and tokenisers. They therefore test whether the
invariance effect generalises beyond one backbone, which is important for RQ2 and RQ3. Prior work
links cross-lingual consistency to subword-vocabulary
overlap~\cite{Qi_2023,reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm}.

\paragraph{Scoring head.}
For a claim--trace input $x_i$, the model produces a relevance score $s_i$. The default
cross-entropy head produces two logits,
$(\ell^{\text{neg}}_i,\ell^{\text{pos}}_i)$. The corresponding probability that the trace is
relevant is
\[
p_i =
\mathrm{softmax}
\left(\ell^{\text{neg}}_i,\ell^{\text{pos}}_i\right)_{\text{pos}}.
\]
For ranking, we use the logit margin
\[
s_i=\ell^{\text{pos}}_i-\ell^{\text{neg}}_i,
\]
which gives the same ordering as the positive-class probability. The alternative binary
cross-entropy head produces one logit $\ell_i$. It uses $s_i=\ell_i$ and
$p_i=\sigma(\ell_i)$.

Both heads score each trace independently of the positions of other candidates. The continuous scores
support both within-claim ranking and cross-lingual alignment.

\paragraph{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning.}
We fine-tune each backbone using QLoRA~\cite{dettmers2023qloraefficientfinetuningquantized,
hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge}. The base model is loaded in 4-bit NF4 precision with a
\texttt{bf16} computation type, and its original parameters remain frozen. Trainable low-rank
adapters are added to all linear projections using
\texttt{target\_modules=all-linear}. Unless stated otherwise, the adapters use rank $r=16$, scaling
factor $\alpha=32$, dropout $0.05$, and no adapter bias.

The classification head remains outside the quantised model and is trained in full precision by
setting \texttt{modules\_to\_save} to \texttt{["score"]}. The new head must learn the relevance-score
scale. PyTorch scaled dot-product attention and gradient checkpointing reduce memory use for long
claim--evidence--trace inputs.

This configuration fits 7--8B models within the available multi-GPU budget. It also leaves most
pre-trained multilingual representations unchanged, allowing us to test whether their shared latent
structure can support invariant scoring
\cite{wendler2024llamasworkenglishlatent,schut2025multilingualllmsthinkenglish}.

\paragraph{Input construction.}
Figures~\ref{fig:supervised-trace-scorer} and~\ref{fig:supervised-trace-scorer-pipeline} show the full
scoring process. Each claim--trace pair becomes one input:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Claim: \{claim\}\\
Evidence snippets:\\
{}[0] \{evidence snippet\}\\
{}[1] \{evidence snippet\}\\
\dots\\
Verdict: \{trace verdict\}\\
Justification: \{cleaned trace justification\}
\end{quote}

We remove ``Label:'' and ``Justification:'' markers, repeated verdict tokens, and unnecessary
whitespace from the justification. The verdict remains in its dedicated field. Removing duplicate
label text prevents it from dominating the reasoning.

We limit the claim, each evidence snippet, the justification, and the number of evidence snippets.
The final sequence is truncated to the configured token limit. The compact setting uses two evidence
snippets and at most $1{,}024$ tokens. The long-context setting uses up to twenty snippets, up to
$3{,}078$ characters per snippet, and at most $15{,}000$ tokens. We exclude justifications shorter
than three words because they contain too little reasoning for training.

\subsection{Trace-Classification Objective}
\label{subsec:method-objective}

The trace scorer is a binary classifier. For trace $i$ of claim $c$, the target is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:label}
z_i=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } v_i=y^\star_c,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A trace is relevant when its verdict matches the gold label. The two-logit head uses cross-entropy
between $(\ell^{\text{neg}}_i,\ell^{\text{pos}}_i)$ and $z_i$. The single-logit head uses binary
cross-entropy between $\sigma(\ell_i)$ and $z_i$. We call this term the ranking loss,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}$, because it trains the scores used for ranking.

At inference, traces are scored independently and sorted in decreasing score order:
\[
r(c)=\operatorname{argsort}_{i\in R(c)}(-s_i).
\]
When two traces receive the same score, their original trace indices are used to break the tie
deterministically.

The model does not use a separate head for the claim verdict. Instead, it takes a majority vote over
the top-$k$ trace verdicts, with $k=5$ by default:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:verdict}
\hat{y}_c=
\operatorname{majority}
\bigl(\{\,v_i \mid i\in r_{1:k}(c)\,\}\bigr).
\end{equation}
If the vote is tied, the highest-ranked tied label wins. The learned trace scores therefore determine
both the ranking and the claim verdict, so they can affect Recall@5 and macro-F1.

\subsection{Language-Invariance Objective}
\label{subsec:method-invariance}

The main methodological contribution is a score-level regulariser added to
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}$. It penalises different relevance profiles for corresponding traces
across languages. Unlike objectives defined only over discrete verdicts, it acts on continuous trace
scores. It follows prior cross-lingual consistency work
\cite{zheng2021consistencyregularizationcrosslingualfinetuning,wang2025calmunleashingcrosslingualselfaligning,liu2026posttraininglanguagemodelscrosslingual}
but applies the idea directly to ranking, so its effect on ranking quality can be measured (RQ1).
Figure~\ref{fig:language-invariance} gives an overview.

\paragraph{Aligned multilingual batching.}
We build trace features separately for each language and tag them with a claim identifier
(\texttt{dataset\_index}), trace index, and language identifier. For every claim available in all
languages, we keep the trace indices that survive preprocessing everywhere. These examples form an
\emph{aligned claim group}. Batches are made from complete groups, ensuring that the invariance loss
has all required parallel scores. Claims with no common trace provide no invariance signal and are
removed from the aligned set, although they may still contribute to $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}$.
Classification labels and metadata remain unchanged, so the ranking loss matches the monolingual case.

\paragraph{Score normalisation.}
Absolute score scales may differ across languages even when the relative ordering is identical.
Because ranking depends only on within-claim relative scores, we remove the per-claim, per-language
location and scale. For claim $c$, language $\ell$, and common trace set $T_c$, let
$\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}_c=(s^{(\ell)}_{c,i})_{i\in T_c}$. We standardise it as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:znorm}
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{(\ell)}_c=
\frac{\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}_c-\operatorname{mean}\bigl(\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}_c\bigr)}
     {\operatorname{std}\bigl(\mathbf{s}^{(\ell)}_c\bigr)+\varepsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ provides numerical stability. Standardisation removes harmless constant offsets
and focuses on the shape of the score profile: which trace ranks above which and by how much relative
to the spread. This aligns the regulariser with ranking behaviour.

\paragraph{Invariance loss.}
For each aligned claim with at least two languages, we compute the mean squared difference between the
standardised score vectors for every language pair and average over claims:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:invloss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}=
\operatorname{mean}_{c}\;
\frac{1}{\binom{|\mathcal{L}_c|}{2}}
\sum_{\ell<\ell'}
\bigl\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{(\ell)}_c-\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{(\ell')}_c \bigr\rVert_2^2 \big/ |T_c|,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_c$ is the set of languages available for claim $c$. The total training objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}+\lambda_{\text{inv}}(t)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}},
\end{equation}
where the weight $\lambda_{\text{inv}}(t)$ increases linearly from $0$ to its target during the first
fraction $\rho$ of the training steps and then remains constant:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warmup}
\lambda_{\text{inv}}(t)=
\begin{cases}
\lambda^\star\cdot \dfrac{t}{\rho\,T}, & t<\rho T,\\[1.2ex]
\lambda^\star, & t\ge \rho T,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the total number of optimisation steps. Warm-up lets the head learn a useful score scale
from $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}$ before invariance pressure begins. Applying both losses from the
first step could collapse the scores into a consistent but uninformative solution
\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023}. Setting $\lambda^\star=0$ recovers
the standard multilingual scorer, providing an ablation with the same data, batching, and
architecture. We log $\mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}$, and
$\lambda_{\text{inv}}(t)$ separately.

\subsection{Value-Aware Numerical Components}
\label{subsec:method-numeric}

The same quantity can have different surface forms across languages. It may use Western Arabic,
Arabic-Indic, or Persian digits, as well as different decimal and thousands separators. These forms
can produce different tokenisations and contribute to cross-lingual
inconsistency~\cite{reddy2026effectscriptsformatsllm,
wallace2019nlpmodelsknownumbers,
bhattacharya2025investigatinginteractionlinguisticmathematical}. To reduce this variation, we
use two optional value-aware components. Both work with the standard scorer and the invariance
objective.

\paragraph{Numeric canonicalisation.}
Both components use the same numeric canonicalisation. A regular expression identifies Western
Arabic, Arabic-Indic, and Persian digits, percentage and per-mille signs, and numbers with comma or
decimal separators. Detected expressions are converted to ASCII digits. Their separators are
interpreted and rewritten in one decimal format, while percentage and per-mille expressions are
converted to decimal values. Each result is parsed and rendered as a canonical decimal string.

Different scripts and separator conventions can therefore map to the same value. The two components
use this canonical form differently.

\paragraph{Value-aware numeric embeddings.}
Figure~\ref{fig:numeric-embedding} shows the value-aware embedding pipeline. A special
\texttt{<num>} token is inserted before each detected number, while the original number remains in
the text. A small learned encoder represents the canonical value character by character using digits,
a sign, a decimal point, and an exponent marker. A single-layer GRU processes these character
embeddings and projects them to the backbone's hidden size.

At each \texttt{<num>} position, the value vector replaces the standard token embedding. The
transformer therefore receives both the explicit value and the original surface form. This reduces
dependence on numeral tokenisation and follows previous work on value-aware numerical
representations~\cite{dutulescu2026valueawarenumericalrepresentationstransformer,
schwartz2024numerologicnumberencodingenhanced}.

The \texttt{<num>} token is added to the tokeniser, and the input embedding matrix is resized. The
value encoder is trained with the LoRA adapters and classification head and saved with the trained
adapter for inference.

\caption{Numeric embedding pipeline. Numerical spans are canonicalized, marked with \texttt{<num>}, encoded with a GRU value encoder, and inserted as value-aware embeddings before trace scoring.}

\paragraph{Plain-text numeric normalisation.}
The second component exposes canonical values without changing the embedding layer. It appends a
plain-text section headed
\texttt{Normalized numbers}, to the trace-scorer input. This section lists the numbers found in the
claim, evidence, and reasoning trace. When a number's original and canonical forms differ, it is
shown as a \texttt{surface=canonical} pair.

Because the normalised values are ordinary text, the architecture remains unchanged. This provides a
lightweight alternative to the learned encoder and tests whether simply showing canonical values is
enough. The procedure ignores small leading integers used as list markers, such as ``1.'' or ``2)'',
so they are not mistaken for quantities.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{subsec:method-baselines}

The shared-task baselines provide reference points for both ranking quality and invariance.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prompt baseline.} A frozen instruction-tuned backbone is the untrained reference. Given
a claim, evidence, and candidate traces, it directly produces a ranking and verdict. We use both
listwise and pairwise prompts.

\item \textbf{Listwise prompting} (Figure~\ref{fig:listwise-prompted-ranking}). One prompt contains
the claim, evidence, and every trace. The model returns strict JSON with a permutation of trace
indices and one verdict. The ranking remains sensitive to the input order despite an instruction not
to copy it, reproducing known position
bias~\cite{qiao2026llmbasedlistwisererankingeffect,shi2025judgingjudgessystematicstudy}.

\item \textbf{Pairwise prompting} (Figure~\ref{fig:pairwise-prompted-ranking}). The model compares two
traces at a time, and accumulated wins define the ranking
\cite{qin2024largelanguagemodelseffective}. A \emph{balanced} orientation alternates which trace
appears first. A \emph{bidirectional} orientation evaluates both orders and counts a win only when
they agree. These methods reduce but do not remove order effects. They require $n(n-1)/2$ and
$n(n-1)$ calls per claim, respectively.

\item \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)} (Figures~\ref{fig:dpo-pipeline}
and~\ref{fig:dpo-ranking}). An English LoRA model starts from a supervised Mistral-7B adapter and is
trained on automatically created preference pairs
\cite{rafailov2024directpreferenceoptimizationlanguage}. The chosen output ranks gold-matching traces
first and predicts the gold verdict. Hard negatives either place a non-matching trace first, swap the
top traces, or use an incorrect verdict. DPO operates on complete generated rankings rather than
per-trace scores. It therefore retains order and format sensitivity and does not directly optimise
Recall@5.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:method-eval}

We report task quality and language invariance \emph{separately}. Their relationship is therefore an
empirical result rather than part of the definition
\cite{elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained,Qi_2023} (RQ2).

\paragraph{Task metrics.}
For each language, we report Recall@5 and MRR@5 over gold-matching traces, verdict macro-F1, and the
official mean of macro-F1 and Recall@5. We also report the Recall@5 upper bound. Multilingual runs
give each metric per language and as a macro-average across languages.

\paragraph{Verdict invariance.}
We treat the language-conditioned verdicts
$\{\hat{y}_\ell(c)\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}$ as raters of the same claim. Agreement measures are the
fraction of claims on which all languages agree, Fleiss' $\kappa$ across languages
\cite{fleiss1971measuring}, pairwise Cohen's $\kappa$~\cite{cohen1960coefficient}, and
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ for missing variants~\cite{krippendorff2004content}. Only aligned claims are
compared.

\paragraph{Ranking invariance.}
Ranking agreement uses mean pairwise Kendall's $\tau$, Spearman's
$\rho$~\cite{kendall1938new,spearman1904proof}, and top-$k$ Jaccard overlap. The measures compare trace
indices common to both orderings. The same evaluation also compares original and translated variants,
including validation sets with mismatched claim and trace languages. These checks help separate the
effects of claim language, trace language, and translation noise.

\paragraph{Training-time invariance.}
On held-out data, the evaluator also reports $\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}$: the mean standardised score
MSE from Equation~\eqref{eq:invloss}, overall and by language pair. Comparing this training target with
$\kappa$ and $\tau$ shows whether lower score disagreement produces verdict- and ranking-level
invariance without reducing task quality~\cite{ifergan2024beneathsurfaceconsistencyexploring}.

\subsection{Experimental Configuration}
\label{subsec:method-config}

\paragraph{Training details.}
Unless stated otherwise, trace scorers train for five epochs with AdamW, a learning rate of
$1\!\times\!10^{-4}$, a learning-rate warm-up ratio of $0.03$, per-device batch size up to $8$ with
gradient accumulation, and bf16 compute. Early stopping uses positive-class F1 for single-language
runs and the macro-F1/Recall@5 mean for multilingual runs. We save the adapter from the best
evaluation step, not the final step. Invariance runs use $\rho=0.2$ and sweep $\lambda^\star$, always
including $\lambda^\star=0$ as the control. The \emph{regularised} setting uses adapter dropout
$0.10$ and weight decay $0.01$. Every configuration uses three random seeds, and we report their mean.
Weights and Biases records the experiments and metrics.

\paragraph{Compute.}
Experiments run on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Main trace-scorer training uses two GPUs with distributed data
parallelism through \texttt{torchrun}, inference and smaller ablations use one GPU. All LLM scorers
load the base model at 4-bit precision, use QLoRA adapters and SDPA attention, and enable CUDA
expandable memory segments for long inputs.

\paragraph{Experiment matrix.}
The backbones have different roles. \textbf{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3} covers all shared-task ranking
formulations and serves as a reference, it is not extended with the invariance objective.
\textbf{Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct} is the primary backbone. It covers prompted baselines, DPO,
multilingual trace scoring, long-context and regularised variants, numeric normalisation, and the
invariance objective. \textbf{Qwen3-8B} uses the matching prompted baseline, the best Llama ranking
setting (multilingual, long-context, and regularised), numeric normalisation, and invariance settings.
This design separates the objective's effect from one model family's behaviour and compares English,
Spanish, and the lower-resource, different-script Arabic setting (RQ2 and RQ3). The results section
reports task quality and cross-lingual consistency separately before examining their relationship.
